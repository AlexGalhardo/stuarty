Terms on this page: 29. Words on a video help people who cannot hear, people who watch with the sound off and people who speak another language.

### Caption

Captions write down the speech and also important sounds, like [laughter]. They help people who are deaf, and everybody who watches without sound.

**Example.** On a train, you watch a video with the sound off, and captions let you follow the story.

**In Vukem Video.** A caption is word-timed text on a caption track. See [Text and captions](/docs/guide/text-and-captions).

**See also:** [Subtitle](#subtitle), [Transcript](#transcript), [Word timing](#word-timing).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Closed captioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_captioning) · [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative: captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

### Closed captions

Closed captions are a separate track of text. The player draws them, and the viewer chooses to show them (the CC button).

**Example.** You press CC on a streaming service to read the words.

**Also called:** CC. **See also:** [Open captions](#open-captions), [SRT](#srt).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Closed captioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_captioning)

### Credits

Credits thank the people and name the roles. In movies they roll up the screen at the end.

**Example.** The names scroll upwards after the film is over.

**Also called:** end credits, credit roll. **See also:** [Title](#title).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Closing credits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closing_credits)

### Drop shadow

A shadow makes text stand out from the picture. A light, small shadow looks best.

**Example.** A title that seems to float above the video.

**See also:** [Stroke](#stroke).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Drop shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drop_shadow)

### Dubbing

Dubbing records new voices in another language and puts them in place of the original ones. Subtitles instead keep the original voices.

**Example.** A cartoon spoken in French, dubbed into Portuguese.

**See also:** [Subtitle](#subtitle), [Voice-over](/docs/glossary/audio#voice-over).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Dubbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubbing)

### Font

A font decides how letters look. Some are plain, some playful, some formal.

**Example.** A birthday card in a curly font looks friendlier than one in a strict font.

**In Vukem Video.** Import your own with `font_import`, then add it with `font.add`. See [Brand kit](/docs/guide/brand-kit).

**Also called:** typeface. **See also:** [Serif and sans-serif](#serif-and-sans-serif), [Font weight](#font-weight).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Font](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Font) · [Wikipedia: Typeface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface)

### Font weight

Weight makes letters thicker or thinner. Bold stands out, light is calm.

**Example.** A title in bold and a note in light.

**See also:** [Font](#font).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Font](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Font)

### Kerning

Some pairs of letters, like A and V, look too far apart. Kerning moves them closer so the word looks even.

**Example.** The gap between A and V in "AVA" is made smaller.

**See also:** [Leading](#leading), [Letter spacing](#letter-spacing).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Kerning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerning)

### Leading

Leading (pronounced "ledding") is how far apart two lines are. Too tight and lines touch; too loose and they look like separate sentences.

**Example.** A caption in two lines that are easy to read.

**Also called:** line spacing. **See also:** [Line break](#line-break), [Kerning](#kerning).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Leading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading)

### Letter spacing

Wider spacing looks airy and calm. Tighter spacing looks dense and strong.

**Example.** Small capital words look good with wider spacing.

**Also called:** tracking. **See also:** [Kerning](#kerning).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Letter-spacing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter-spacing)

### Line break

Good captions break lines at natural places, like after a comma, so each line is easy to read in one glance.

**Example.** "I love my new" / "red bicycle" is better than "I love" / "my new red bicycle".

**In Vukem Video.** `caption.set_breaks` chooses where a caption breaks.

**See also:** [Reading speed](#reading-speed), [Leading](#leading).

### Lower third

A lower third is text in the lower third of the picture, like a person's name when they first speak.

**Example.** A news report shows "Ana Silva, Scientist" under a person.

**See also:** [Title](#title), [Safe area](#safe-area).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Lower third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_third)

### Open captions

Open (burned-in) captions are part of the video image. Every viewer sees them, on every app. They cannot be switched off or changed.

**Example.** Short videos for social networks often have big words burned in.

**In Vukem Video.** Captions in Vukem Video are burned in when you export.

**Also called:** burned-in captions, hardcoded subtitles. **See also:** [Closed captions](#closed-captions).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Closed captioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_captioning)

### Reading speed

Captions need to stay on screen long enough to be read. About 15 to 20 characters per second is a common limit for adults.

**Example.** A sentence that flashes by in half a second is too fast.

**See also:** [Caption](#caption), [Line break](#line-break).

**Learn more:** [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative: captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

### Safe area

Apps put buttons and names over the video, and some screens cut the edges. Keeping text inside the safe area makes sure people can read it.

**Example.** On a short video the bottom is covered by the caption and the heart button, so the words go higher.

**In Vukem Video.** The lint checks text against the safe area. See [Safe area](/docs/guide/safe-area).

**See also:** [Vertical video](/docs/glossary/shooting-and-footage#vertical-video), [Lower third](#lower-third).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Safe area (television)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_area_(television))

### Serif and sans-serif

A serif font has small strokes at the ends of letters, like in a book. Sans-serif ("without feet") is cleaner and is common on screens.

**Example.** Books use serif letters; most phone apps use sans-serif.

**See also:** [Font](#font).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Serif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serif) · [Wikipedia: Sans-serif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sans-serif)

### Speaker label

When people talk off screen, or two voices overlap, a label like "ANA:" tells the viewer who speaks.

**Example.** "MOM: Dinner is ready!" in the captions.

**See also:** [Caption](#caption).

**Learn more:** [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative: captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

### SRT

An SRT file is plain text. Each block has a number, a time range and the words. Almost every player can read it.

**Example.** "1", "00:00:01,000 --&gt; 00:00:03,000", "Hello!".

**Also called:** SubRip. **See also:** [WebVTT](#webvtt), [Closed captions](#closed-captions).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: SubRip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SubRip)

### Stroke

A stroke is a thin border around the letters. A white text with a dark outline is readable on both dark and light pictures.

**Example.** White letters with a black outline over a bright sky.

**Also called:** outline. **See also:** [Drop shadow](#drop-shadow), [Contrast](/docs/glossary/color#contrast).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Stroke (typography)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke_(typography))

### Subtitle

Subtitles usually write what people say, often in another language. They assume the viewer can hear the sound.

**Example.** A cartoon in Japanese with English words at the bottom.

**See also:** [Caption](#caption), [Dubbing](#dubbing).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Subtitle (captioning)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtitle_(captioning)) · [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative: captions and subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)

### Text alignment

Centre alignment is common for captions and titles. Left alignment is common for paragraphs.

**Example.** Captions are usually centred under the picture.

**Also called:** justification. **See also:** [Line break](#line-break).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Typographic alignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographic_alignment)

### Text animation

Text animation can fade, pop, slide or typewriter the words. It catches attention but it should not make text hard to read.

**Example.** A title that pops in with a bounce.

**In Vukem Video.** `text.apply_preset` has six presets. See [Effects and color](/docs/guide/effects-and-color).

**See also:** [Keyframe](/docs/glossary/effects-and-compositing#keyframe), [Easing](/docs/glossary/cuts-and-transitions#easing).

### Title

A title card or title text names the video, a chapter or a place. It is different from captions, which write the speech.

**Example.** "Summer 2026" appears at the start of a holiday video.

**In Vukem Video.** `text.add`.

**See also:** [Lower third](#lower-third), [Credits](#credits).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Title card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_card)

### Transcript

A transcript is a written copy of the speech. It can be with or without times.

**Example.** The written version of a recorded interview.

**See also:** [Transcription](#transcription), [Caption](#caption).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Transcription (service)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(service))

### Transcription

A transcription program listens to the audio and writes the words with the time when each one is spoken.

**Example.** Your voice message turned into text.

**In Vukem Video.** `vukem-video transcribe` and `caption.from_transcript`. See [Transcription](/docs/guide/transcription).

**Also called:** speech to text. **See also:** [Transcript](#transcript), [Word timing](#word-timing).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition)

### TTML

TTML is an XML format for timed text, with detailed positions and colours. Streaming and TV use it.

**Example.** Captions delivered to a TV channel.

**Also called:** Timed Text Markup Language. **See also:** [WebVTT](#webvtt), [SRT](#srt).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Timed Text Markup Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timed_Text_Markup_Language) · [W3C: Timed Text Markup Language 2 (TTML2)](https://www.w3.org/TR/ttml2/)

### Unicode

Thanks to Unicode a video editor can write Chinese, Portuguese accents and emoji in the same title.

**Example.** The letter "ã" and the emoji "😀" both have a Unicode number.

**See also:** [Font](#font).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode)

### WebVTT

WebVTT is like SRT with extras for position and style. It works with the video tag in web pages.

**Example.** A website that shows captions under its own video player.

**Also called:** VTT. **See also:** [SRT](#srt).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: WebVTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WebVTT) · [W3C: WebVTT, the Web Video Text Tracks format](https://www.w3.org/TR/webvtt1/)

### Word timing

With word timing each word knows when it is said. This lets the caption highlight the word being spoken, like a karaoke screen.

**Example.** In a short video the current word turns yellow as the person says it.

**In Vukem Video.** Every caption word has its own start and length.

**See also:** [Caption](#caption), [Transcription](#transcription).
