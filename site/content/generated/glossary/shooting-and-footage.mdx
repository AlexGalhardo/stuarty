Terms on this page: 42. These are the words people use before editing starts: the camera, the shot and the raw video you bring in.

### Aperture

The aperture is a hole in the lens that gets wider or narrower, like the pupil of your eye. A wider hole lets in more light and makes the background blurrier.

**Example.** In a dark room the camera opens its aperture wide to get enough light.

**Also called:** f-stop. **See also:** [Depth of field](#depth-of-field), [Exposure](#exposure).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture)

### Aspect ratio

The aspect ratio is width and height written as two numbers, like 16:9. A big first number means wide, like a TV. A big second number means tall, like a phone held upright.

**Example.** A YouTube video is usually 16:9 (wide). A TikTok is 9:16 (tall).

**In Stuarty.** The presets are 9:16 (the default), 16:9, 1:1 and 4:5. `settings.reframe` changes it. See [Projects](/docs/projects).

**See also:** [Resolution](#resolution), [Vertical video](#vertical-video).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Aspect ratio (image)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_(image))

### Camera angle

The angle is where you put the camera. Looking up at someone makes them seem big and strong. Looking down makes them seem small.

**Example.** Filming a puppy from the floor makes the puppy look like a hero.

**See also:** [Dutch angle](#dutch-angle), [Point of view](#point-of-view).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Camera angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_angle)

### Close-up

A close-up shows one small thing very big, like a face, a hand or an eye. It tells the audience: look at this.

**Example.** A close-up of a hand reaching for the last cookie.

**See also:** [Wide shot](#wide-shot), [Insert shot](#insert-shot).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Close-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-up)

### Depth of field

With a shallow depth of field only the thing you focus on is sharp and the background is soft and blurry. With a deep one, everything is sharp.

**Example.** A portrait where the person is sharp and the trees behind are a soft blur.

**See also:** [Aperture](#aperture), [Focus](#focus).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Depth of field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_of_field)

### Dutch angle

Tilt the camera sideways and everything looks crooked. Filmmakers do it to make you feel nervous or dizzy.

**Example.** In a scary movie, the picture tilts when the monster walks in.

**Also called:** Dutch tilt, canted angle. **See also:** [Camera angle](#camera-angle).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Dutch angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_angle)

### Establishing shot

An establishing shot shows the place first, so the viewer knows where they are before the people appear.

**Example.** A picture of a school building, then a cut to the classroom inside.

**See also:** [Wide shot](#wide-shot), [Scene](#scene).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Establishing shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Establishing_shot)

### Exposure

Exposure is the amount of light that reaches the camera. Too little and the picture is too dark. Too much and it turns white.

**Example.** Filming a friend in front of a bright window turns them into a dark shape: the exposure is for the window.

**See also:** [Aperture](#aperture), [ISO](#iso), [Shutter speed](#shutter-speed).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Exposure (photography)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_(photography))

### Focal length

A small focal length (like 14 mm) sees a wide view. A big one (like 200 mm) sees a narrow, far-away view.

**Example.** A phone's "wide" camera has a smaller focal length than its "telephoto" camera.

**See also:** [Zoom](#zoom), [Depth of field](#depth-of-field).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Focal length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_length)

### Focus

When something is in focus its edges are clear. Cameras can focus by themselves (autofocus) or you can turn a ring by hand.

**Example.** The picture starts blurry and becomes sharp when the camera finds the face.

**Also called:** autofocus. **See also:** [Depth of field](#depth-of-field), [Rack focus](#rack-focus).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Focus (optics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_(optics))

### Footage

Footage is everything the camera recorded. It is the raw material you cut, like flour before you bake a cake.

**Example.** You film your dog for ten minutes at the park. Those ten minutes are your footage.

**In Stuarty.** Footage is what you [import into the media library](/docs/guide/media-library). Each file becomes an asset.

**See also:** [Asset](/docs/glossary/editing-and-timeline#asset), [Raw footage](#raw-footage).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Footage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footage)

### Frame

A video is a very fast flip-book. Each page of the flip-book is a frame. Show enough of them each second and the pictures seem to move.

**Example.** A phone video at 30 frames per second has 30 pictures for every second you watch.

**In Stuarty.** Stuarty counts time in frames, and you can write a position as `97f`. See [Time](/docs/concepts/time).

**See also:** [Frame rate](#frame-rate), [Timecode](/docs/glossary/editing-and-timeline#timecode).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Film frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_frame)

### Frame rate

The frame rate is the number of frames shown in one second. More frames make motion look smoother. Cinema uses 24, many phones use 30 or 60.

**Example.** Slow-motion looks smooth because the camera filmed 120 frames each second and the video plays them at 30.

**In Stuarty.** A new project is 30 fps by default; `settings.set_fps` changes it. See [Projects](/docs/projects).

**Also called:** fps, frames per second. **See also:** [Frame](#frame), [Slow motion](#slow-motion).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Frame rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_rate)

### Gimbal

A gimbal tilts and turns by itself to cancel your shaking, so you can walk and still get a smooth shot.

**Example.** Walking through a crowd with a phone on a gimbal and no wobble.

**See also:** [Handheld](#handheld), [Stabilization](#stabilization).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Gimbal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal)

### Handheld

Handheld footage is shot without a stand, so it shakes a bit. It feels real and close, but can make people dizzy if it shakes too much.

**Example.** A video you film by walking and holding your phone.

**See also:** [Tripod](#tripod), [Gimbal](#gimbal), [Stabilization](#stabilization).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Handheld camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handheld_camera)

### Insert shot

An insert shot shows one small thing the story needs, like a clock, a note or a button.

**Example.** A close shot of the note that says "Meet me at 5".

**See also:** [Cutaway](/docs/glossary/cuts-and-transitions#cutaway), [Close-up](#close-up).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Insert (filmmaking)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insert_(filmmaking))

### ISO

A high ISO makes the camera more sensitive to light, so dark scenes look brighter. The price is noise: little coloured speckles.

**Example.** A phone video at night looks grainy because the camera turned the ISO way up.

**Also called:** film speed. **See also:** [Exposure](#exposure), [Noise](#noise).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Film speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_speed)

### Lens

The lens decides how wide the view is and how blurry the background is. A phone has several tiny lenses.

**Example.** Switching from the 1x to the 0.5x camera on a phone changes the lens.

**See also:** [Focal length](#focal-length), [Aperture](#aperture).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Camera lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_lens)

### Medium shot

A medium shot is in between: close enough to see the face and hands, far enough to see a bit of the room.

**Example.** Someone telling a story at the dinner table is usually filmed in a medium shot.

**See also:** [Wide shot](#wide-shot), [Close-up](#close-up).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Medium shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_shot)

### Noise

In a dark scene the camera boosts the signal and also boosts its own mistakes. You see them as colourful sand on the picture.

**Example.** A phone video filmed at night looks like it is covered with sand.

**See also:** [ISO](#iso), [Exposure](#exposure).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Image noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_noise)

### Over-the-shoulder shot

You see the back of one person's head and shoulder, and the face of the person they are talking to.

**Example.** Two friends talking: the camera sits behind one friend and looks at the other.

**Also called:** OTS. **See also:** [Point of view](#point-of-view), [Shot and reverse shot](/docs/glossary/cuts-and-transitions#shot-and-reverse-shot).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Over the shoulder shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over_the_shoulder_shot)

### Pan

To pan, you stay still and swing the camera sideways, like turning your head to follow a car passing by.

**Example.** Panning slowly across the whole class photo, from the first kid to the last.

**See also:** [Tilt](#tilt), [Tracking shot](#tracking-shot).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Panning (camera)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panning_(camera))

### Pixel

A pixel is one tiny coloured square. Your screen is a grid of them, and each shows one colour.

**Example.** Zoom a photo until it looks like blocks. Each block is a pixel.

**See also:** [Resolution](#resolution).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Pixel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel)

### Point of view

In a point-of-view shot the camera is the character's eyes. The viewer sees exactly what they see.

**Example.** A video from a bike helmet camera, showing the road ahead.

**Also called:** POV. **See also:** [Camera angle](#camera-angle), [Over-the-shoulder shot](#over-the-shoulder-shot).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Point-of-view shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-of-view_shot)

### Rack focus

You start with the near object sharp and the far one blurry, then you change the focus so the far one becomes sharp.

**Example.** The flower in front is sharp. The focus slides and now the girl behind it is sharp.

**See also:** [Focus](#focus), [Depth of field](#depth-of-field).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Follow focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Follow_focus)

### Raw footage

Raw footage is video nobody has touched. It still has the mistakes, the waiting and the parts you will throw away.

**Example.** Before your birthday video is cut, the clip with everyone saying "ready, set..." three times is raw footage.

**Also called:** rushes, dailies. **See also:** [Footage](#footage), [Rough cut](/docs/glossary/industry-slang#rough-cut).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Footage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footage)

### Resolution

Resolution counts the pixels in the picture, like 1920 by 1080. More pixels mean more detail, and a bigger file.

**Example.** A photo printed very large looks blurry if it has too few pixels.

**See also:** [Pixel](#pixel), [Aspect ratio](#aspect-ratio), [Bitrate](/docs/glossary/formats-codecs-containers#bitrate).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Display resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Display_resolution)

### Scene

A scene is a piece of the story that happens in one place at one time. It is usually built from several shots.

**Example.** Everything that happens in the kitchen while you bake is one scene, filmed in many shots.

**See also:** [Shot](#shot), [Sequence](/docs/glossary/editing-and-timeline#sequence).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Scene (filmmaking)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_(filmmaking))

### Sensor

The sensor is a grid of tiny light detectors. A bigger sensor catches more light, so pictures are cleaner in the dark.

**Example.** A big camera filmed the dark party better than a small phone because its sensor is bigger.

**See also:** [Noise](#noise), [Pixel](#pixel).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Image sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_sensor)

### Shot

A shot starts when the camera starts and ends when the camera stops (or when you cut away). A video is many shots in a row.

**Example.** Zooming slowly on a birthday cake until the candles fill the screen is one shot.

**See also:** [Take](#take), [Scene](#scene), [Clip](/docs/glossary/editing-and-timeline#clip).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Shot (filmmaking)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_(filmmaking))

### Shutter speed

A fast shutter freezes motion. A slow shutter leaves a smear. For video, a common rule is to use about double the frame rate (1/48 s for 24 fps).

**Example.** A short shutter freezes the water drops of a splash. A long one makes them a smooth veil.

**See also:** [Motion blur](/docs/glossary/effects-and-compositing#motion-blur), [Frame rate](#frame-rate).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Shutter speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shutter_speed)

### Slate

The slate (or clapperboard) shows the scene and take number. It is filmed first so that, later, the editor can tell the takes apart.

**Example.** Someone holds a board, says "scene 4, take 2" and snaps the clapper.

**Also called:** clapperboard, clapper. **See also:** [Take](#take).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Clapperboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clapperboard)

### Slow motion

Film many frames each second (like 120) and play them at a normal speed (like 30): everything looks four times slower.

**Example.** A balloon popping, shown slowly so you see the rubber tear.

**Also called:** slo-mo. **See also:** [Frame rate](#frame-rate), [Speed ramp](/docs/glossary/cuts-and-transitions#speed-ramp), [Time-lapse](#time-lapse).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Slow motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_motion)

### Stabilization

Stabilization removes shaking. Some cameras do it while filming. Editors can do it afterwards by moving and cropping each frame a little.

**Example.** A bike video that was bumpy looks smoother after stabilization, but a bit more zoomed-in.

**See also:** [Handheld](#handheld), [Gimbal](#gimbal).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Image stabilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_stabilization)

### Take

When a shot goes wrong, you film it again. Each try is a take, usually counted "take one, take two...".

**Example.** Your friend laughs in the middle of saying a line, so you do take two.

**See also:** [Shot](#shot), [Slate](#slate).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Take (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_(film))

### Tilt

A tilt is a pan's cousin: the camera nods up or down instead of turning sideways.

**Example.** Tilting up from a skater's shoes to their face.

**See also:** [Pan](#pan).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Tilt (camera)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilt_(camera))

### Time-lapse

A time-lapse takes one picture every few seconds or minutes. When you play them quickly, slow things like clouds seem to rush.

**Example.** A flower opening in ten seconds when it really took a whole day.

**See also:** [Slow motion](#slow-motion), [Frame rate](#frame-rate).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Time-lapse photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-lapse_photography)

### Tracking shot

In a tracking shot the camera moves, on wheels or on a person's feet, and follows the action.

**Example.** Walking backwards with a phone while your friend runs toward you.

**Also called:** dolly shot. **See also:** [Pan](#pan), [Gimbal](#gimbal).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Tracking shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracking_shot)

### Tripod

A tripod keeps the camera steady so the picture does not wobble. Many shots look better on one.

**Example.** Filming a time-lapse of the sunset: you put the camera on a tripod and leave it.

**See also:** [Handheld](#handheld), [Gimbal](#gimbal).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Tripod (photography)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripod_(photography))

### Vertical video

Vertical video is taller than wide, usually 9:16. It fills a phone screen, so apps like TikTok and Reels use it.

**Example.** A video you film holding the phone upright and watch without turning the phone.

**In Stuarty.** 9:16 is the default frame of a Stuarty project.

**See also:** [Aspect ratio](#aspect-ratio), [Safe area](/docs/glossary/text-and-captions#safe-area).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Vertical video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_video)

### Wide shot

A wide shot is far away. You see the person and the place around them, like a whole football field.

**Example.** A wide shot of a kid on a huge empty beach shows how alone they are.

**Also called:** long shot. **See also:** [Close-up](#close-up), [Establishing shot](#establishing-shot), [Medium shot](#medium-shot).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Long shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_shot)

### Zoom

A zoom makes things look nearer without you moving. Careful: the picture also gets blurrier on a phone, because it just stretches pixels.

**Example.** Zooming in on the birthday candles from across the room.

**See also:** [Focal length](#focal-length).

**Learn more:** [Wikipedia: Zoom lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoom_lens)
